Enriching Ethics Education Through Game Design: A
Demonstration of Student Experience
KAREN SCHRIER, Marist University, United States
RAFAEL DA SILVA, Boise State University, United States
This study explores the feasibility of using game design as a learning approach to allow students to creatively
explore ethical frameworks. Sixteen participants in an undergraduate ethics course were tasked with creating
a game using the game development tool Twine. The game was based on one of three ethical frameworks:
Kantian, Utilitarian, or Virtue Ethics. Most participants designed games using the Utilitarian framework. Stu-
dents’ games primarily employed story-based design techniques like including characters and dilemmas, as
well as choices and outcomes, with all but one game incorporating consequences of players’ actions in their
designs. An analysis of pre- and post-surveys, student game write-ups, and semi-structured interviews sug-
gested that creating games allowed students to reflect on ethical frameworks by considering ethical decision-
making in context and designing consequences based on players’ actions. Designing a game allowed students
to playfully engage with ethical frameworks. We recommend that future studies examine specific processes
in game design that can improve learning effectiveness and increase creative freedom.

1 Introduction
Effective ethics education requires more than the memorization of definitions or the passive ab-
sorption of philosophical theories. To more fully understand an ethical framework, students must
be able to manage ambiguity and consider different perspectives, as well as to experience their ap-
plication to real-world situations. Standard pedagogical practices, however, often struggle to bridge
the gap between theoretical knowledge and practical application in ethics [Barak and Green 2021].
Traditional lectures or static case studies may not fully engage students in the complexity of ethi-
cal decision-making, potentially leading to a superficial understanding where students can define
a framework by rote, but fail to apply it accurately in complex, dynamic scenarios. This suggests a
need for pedagogical approaches that aid learners in actively constructing ethical understanding
through application.

Game design offers one alternative to traditional methods of teaching ethics. Designing a game
requires designers to operationalize abstract concepts into procedural systems of choices and con-
sequences [Bogost 2008; Salen and Zimmerman 2004]. In this study, we analyze how designing
a narrative game might allow students to contextualize and critique ethical frameworks. We uti-
lized Twine 2.0 (hereafter known as Twine), an accessible narrative design tool, to investigate
whether the process of game making facilitates the application of ethical principles. Specifically,
we examine how learners translate ethical frameworks into interactive, playable, decision-making
structures like Twine games. Previous research has suggested that bringing game design to the
classroom is useful, for instance, for helping students to enhance identity exploration [Schrier et
al. 2021], contextualize and practice previous learning [An 2016], and learn project management
and problem-solving skills [Alexander and Ho 2015]. While the use of games to effectively teach
ethics [Schrier 2019] and practice ethical decision-making skills [da Silva 2021] has been reported
in the literature, the use of game design to learn and practice the understanding of ethical frame-
works remains a research area yet to be explored.
We conducted a qualitative study with 16 participants to understand how they used game design
techniques to create a game based on one of three ethical frameworks: Kantian, Utilitarian, or
Virtue Ethics. In this article, we first discuss the relationship between games and ethics, share
literature on game design in instructional contexts, and present previous literature that supports
the use of Twine in educational practices that have game creation as a learning objective. Next, we
share our methodology, results, and analysis, and our recommendations for educators who want
to use games and game design using Twine and other game design platforms for ethics learning
and practice.
2 Literature Review
2.1 Constructionism and Game Design in Instructional Contexts
This study is grounded in the traditions of design-based learning and constructionism, which
suggest that learners construct knowledge most effectively when they actively create, externalize,
and reflect on artifacts of their learning [Kafai and Burke 2015; Papert 1980]. Constructionism
emphasizes “learning-by-making” [Papert 1980], where the act of producing an artifact, such
as a computer program, story, or game, supports deeper understanding because learners must
translate ideas into shareable, testable forms. Unlike constructivism, which emphasizes internal
construction of knowledge, constructionism expands on this and also highlights the social and
material dimensions of making, such as artifacts can be shared, critiqued, and iteratively improved
upon [Ackermann 2001].
Game design represents a potentially powerful constructionist practice because it requires learn-
ers to transform abstract concepts into tangible play by designing elements like mechanics, branch-
ing narratives, and interactive systems [Akcaoglu and Green 2019]. This aligns with Gee and Gee’s
[2017] concept of distributed learning systems, where intelligence is not solely an internal prop-
erty of the learner, but is stretched across the tools and environments. Through a game design
process, learners externalize their thinking, test their assumptions, and refine their understanding
in relation to both the artifact and its audience. For instance, game design can help learners to
explore, through creative production, the perspectives of others’ and one’s own identities [Schrier
2019; Thumlert et al. 2018]. Schrier [2019] studied the effectiveness of game jams for improving the
understanding of one’s identities and the consideration of others’ perspectives. The experience of
creating games about identity helped game jam participants in recognizing “other ways of viewing
the world” (p. 32) and understanding the concept of identity itself. In another study, students who
were tasked with designing games or creating content for games were able to exercise and develop
their literacy skills, including reading, writing, and digital literacy [Owston et al. 2009].

Particularly relevant to our study is the potential of game design in helping learners recognize
“a metastructure in which the game mechanics, interactions, and content are to be embedded”
[Kafai and Burke 2015, p. 321]. In a mixed-methods study, Akcaoglu and Green [2019] analyzed
the benefits of a game design course in fostering middle-schoolers’ problem-solving and systems
thinking. In their quantitative data, the researchers reported that students who developed games
to improve their understanding of systems performed significantly better than those in a control
group. In their qualitative data, Akcaoglu and Green [2019] provide examples that illustrate how
participants negotiated their vision for games with the capabilities and their understanding of the
development tool used in the intervention, Microsoft Kodu, thus also practicing problem-solving
through making design decisions, such as adjusting game scope.
In experiences focused on learning through game design, students are not passive receivers of
information. Rather, they are required to be active throughout the learning process when they act
as designers [Yang and Chang 2013]. For instance, to develop a design project or artifact, learners
need to acquaint themselves with the content knowledge necessary to develop their creations
and use that knowledge to fulfill the task. In the context of this study, understanding an ethical
framework (e.g., utilitarianism) could be conjectured to be a crucial step prior to and during the
students’ development of their games.
Finally, researchers in ethics education have noted the importance of moral sensitivity and
perspective-taking in ethics [May and Luth 2013]. People may not necessarily agree with or uphold
other’s ethical decisions, but ethics education gives students an opportunity to play with other’s
responses and to consider how their own actions might uphold or be different from another’s
perspectives. Given this finding, coupled with the research on game jams, we hypothesized that
making games about and inspired by ethical frameworks might help students comprehend others’
ethical approaches, even if they do not themselves employ them in their own ethical practices.
2.2 Twine 2.0 in Instruction
Twine is a game-creation platform that allows for the creation of text-based interactive games.
Previous studies have underscored the effectiveness of its use for game design in instructional
contexts. Clark and Baxter [2018] report the results of a study where students developed games
about microbiology. More than half of student participants declared that creating a game with
Twine led to an improvement in their confidence for game creation, even without specific guidance
and programming skills. Students’ games were found to integrate science concepts effectively and
creatively. Clark and Baxter [2018]’s study also highlights that Twine can benefit both students
and also instructors who do not have extensive knowledge of game creation.
Twine was also used as a tool in Roine et al. ’s [2021] study, which investigated the use of game
creation as an approach to assessing student learning in a Master’s level course on storytelling
and digital media. In addition to its user-friendly nature, the researchers emphasized the ease of
distributing Twine games within a learning management system (LMS) as these games are
published as HTML files. In their study, games were developed in groups of four or five students
within the context of a short game jam. Roine et al. [2021] found that the experience was effective in
helping students bridge the gap between theory and practice. These results indicate that designing
Twine games might be useful for students who are taking ethical theories (frameworks) and putting
them into practice.
Increased student engagement has also been suggested to be a benefit of using Twine in in-
structional contexts [Thompson 2020]. Thompson’s [2020] intervention, conducted with forensic
ecology students at a British university, adopted a group game design approach. The results of
this study indicated an increased and more consistent impact on understanding in Twine-focused
experiences compared to traditional content lectures.

Friedhoff’s work on Twine as a platform [2013] underscores Twine’s shallow learning curve
(especially compared to more complex and harder-to-learn game creation tools like Unity and
Unreal Engine). Friedhoff notes that the platform’s audience of writers and students, rather than
traditional game makers and coders, shows how it is used by those who do not necessarily have
technical expertise. Though two of the studies discussed in this section centered on the creation
of games in student groups, Twine’s simplicity and flexibility for text-based game design are also
well-suited for individual game creation [Salter 2016]. Thus, we have posited here that the plat-
form’s relative ease-of-use would make it a tool for rapidly developing short games based on ethical
frameworks within the constraints of an undergraduate course.
2.3 Games and Play
Games are digital environments that often enable players to experiment with identities and skills
within the constraints of the designed space [Bogost 2008; Salen and Zimmerman 2004]. Bogost
[2008] argues that games are modes of expression that benefit from procedural rhetoric, “the prac-
tice of using processes persuasively” (p. 125). According to Bogost [2008], in play, individuals ex-
perience a sequence of “rule-based models” (p. 123) within which they can exercise different skills,
styles of play, and identities. Game play can be understood as an exploration of and experimenta-
tion with possibilities in an environment that is simulated and finite in nature [Gee and Gee 2017].
Players interact with a game by performing sequences of meaningful choices [Salen and Zim-
merman 2004]. Performance is, thus, a central component of game play [Gee and Gee 2017]. In
performing within a game, players can make micro choices, that is, single choices made by a player
within the designed system, or macro choices, which are overarching choices that connect and
provide meaning to micro choices [Salen and Zimmerman 2004]. Meaningful play is, according to
Salen and Zimmerman [2004], the result of the careful design of possible choices, which can allow
players to experience, interpret, and reflect upon decisions made and their outcomes in the game
space. Studies with undergraduate students [Iten et al. 2017 2018] indicates that game choices that
include social and moral components can be particularly meaningful for players.
In the context of ethics education, the act of designing a game requires the learner and designer
to construct such rule-based models from scratch. To create a game that offers meaningful choices
[Salen and Zimmerman 2004] about Utilitarian, Virtue, or Kantian Ethics, a student must act as the
architect of the system, and determine how specific player actions (micro choices) might logically
result in ethical consequences (macro outcomes) [Salen and Zimmerman 2004]. We leverage this
relationship by tasking students with the creation of their own procedural rhetoric, which may
prompt them to externalize and operationalize their understanding of such ethical frameworks.
2.4 Games and Ethics
Game designers can employ a variety of strategies to promote moral reflection and ethical think-
ing. One design strategy is to provide players with agency over the game narrative, such as by
including moral dilemmas and ethical decision-making as part of the gameplay, and adjusting
game outcomes based on a player’s decisions [Schrier et al. 2022].
An early notable instance of ethics and morality in a video game is I Have No Mouth and I
Must Scream (1995 video game). In this game, your actions and responses to moral questions can
contribute to or deter you from achieving the final goal of defeating AM, a sentient artificially-
intelligent supercomputer who has “god-like” powers. AM has been controlling the fates of
five surviving characters caught in a type of liminal, purgatory state [Schrier 2014]. Games like
this, in which ethics were incorporated into the play experience, have served as inspirations for
modern-day “ethically notable” [Zagal 2009, p. 7] games (ENGs) that meaningfully incorporate
moral dilemmas.

As gaming technologies have evolved, ENGs have evolved in how they incorporate ethics into
the play experience. Schrier [2017] analyzed how players in Fable III made ethical decisions, such
as whether to preserve a lake, build an orphanage, and in how they treat non-player characters
(NPCs), like their mentor in the game, Walter. Her results suggested that players of games used
a variety of ethics-related thinking skills like reflection, systems thinking, communication with
others, identifying pros and cons, and reasoning through possibilities.
Likewise, Staines et al. [2019] analyzed three ENGs, Firewatch, Deus Ex: Mankind Divided, and
Darkest Dungeon, and their results suggested that games are becoming more nuanced in how they
incorporate ethical decision-making, such as by using both larger and smaller (micro) questions,
and by provoking more difficult conundrums and reflections on previous decisions. These games
allow players to explore the game space, the characters, and/or the narrative by making smaller-
scale, everyday decisions. These decisions can be presented through, for instance, “social battles,”
as in the case of Deus Ex, or the management of the wellbeing of heroes, such as in Darkest Dungeon.
This is similar to more recent games like Animal Crossing: New Horizons, Spiritfarer, and Stardew
Valley, where ethical questions are subtler and involve shepherding NPCs, supporting their needs,
and engaging in pastoral care [Glaser et al. 2024; Schrier et al. 2019].
The process of using different strategies to incorporate ethics and morality into game design may
also encourage players to reflect on ethical frameworks and engage in ethical decision-making.
Schrier’s [2015] EPIC framework, which draws from previous ethics and games frameworks as
well as ENGs, introduces several strategies that educators can use to select games and achieve ed-
ucational goals, such as enhancing ethical awareness, reflection, and practicing ethical reasoning.
Among the strategies identified, especially relevant for this investigation are: emotion, mood, and
tone, role-playing; choices and consequences; application to real-world issues; and procedural ex-
ploration and interaction. Though EPIC focuses on principles to how to use games for instruction,
this framework illustrates how game creation also may be used to contextualize complex ethical
decision-making. EPIC is not a central framework for our study, but it highlights important prin-
ciples that could be used for game design such as choices and consequences, role-playing, and
reflection, and which align with the affordances of Twine. That said, EPIC helps to illustrate why
using games for ethics (and game design itself) might be appropriate tools for helping students to
externalize ethical reasoning and reflection.
3 Methodology
For this study, we assigned a game design activity in a course on ethics, which fulfills the uni-
versity’s ethics and justice core requirement. The course is called Ethics and Gaming, and it uses
games, game design, and discussions around gaming to teach the basics of ethics, ethical thinking,
and the building of ethical arguments. The course that is described in this study included only
undergraduate students and a mix of majors, including ones in computer science, communication,
games, marketing, and business. Most students were juniors or seniors, and none were Philosophy
or Ethics majors. Only students who volunteered for the study were included in this study.
The game design activity we studied involved students individually designing one short interac-
tive game using Twine (Twine 2.0). Our primary aim in this study was to understand the potential
of Twine as a pedagogical tool for ethics education, by investigating how students used it in an
ethics education context. Specifically, we examined how students used Twine’s capabilities, such
as its enabling of the development of branching narrative games, and how this might support their
understanding of ethical frameworks. Our central aim is to highlight Twine’s value for instructors
seeking accessible ways to integrate design-based learning into ethics curricula. To capture stu-
dents’ experiences in designing games for ethics and assess the efficacy of game design to foster
understanding of three ethical frameworks, we addressed the following questions in this study:

3.1 Assignment Overview and Procedures
The first author served as the instructor for the course. Students were first provided instruction
on three frameworks (Utilitarianism, Virtue Ethics, Kantian Ethics) in three successive weeks of
the course. Prior to these weeks of instruction, they also learned Hedonism, but this was not used
as part of this particular game assignment. The pedagogical instruction that was provided prior
to the Twine game assignment included overviews of each framework (e.g., major tenets, related
philosophers) and class discussions on the strengths and drawbacks of each framework. Students
were assigned relevant chapters in The Fundamentals of Ethics textbook [Schafer-Landau, 2021] to
read prior to each week’s discussions. Students were also taught how to specifically apply these
frameworks to gaming through discussions about games and through a series of short, collabo-
rative hands-on exercises done in pairs in class. For example, students played ethically notable
[Zagal 2009] games such Papers, Please [Pope 2013] and discussed how we might apply utilitarian
frameworks to them, and what types of design techniques in the games might or might not align
with utilitarianism.
After the weekly lessons that were focused on the three ethical frameworks, students were then
coached on how to use the narrative game creation tool Twine. This step involved direct instruc-
tion as well as a guided full-class design effort in which the instructor collaboratively designed
a short game in Twine with the students. Next, students were given an assignment sheet for a
graded project where they needed to individually design a game that reflects one of three ethical
frameworks (Kantian, Utilitarian, Virtue Ethics). Students developed part of the first draft of their
game during class and then finished the first draft out-of-class over a week-long period. They also
had another in-class period to continue to work on the final draft of the game, and another week
to further develop it out-of-class. All students in the class also needed to write a reflection essay
as part of the assignment, which described how the game applied their chosen ethical framework,
and their design approaches and techniques. Thus, this reflection paper externalized their thought
processes on how they designed the game.
In class, after their first game draft was complete, students “playtested” their creations by ob-
serving other classmates playing their game. Students who playtested gave critical feedback on
strengths (what worked) and weaknesses (what did not work) in the game, its story, and any other
components. Critiques could be thematic (e.g., the ending, story structure) or functional (e.g., un-
intentional dead ends, bugs). They also needed to comment on how well the ethical framework
was operationalized within the game’s design.
3.2 Data Collection Instruments and Procedures
This study was first approved by the lead author’s institution’s IRB (#S22-025). All students in
the course completed the graded assignment in which data was collected, but only 16 of the 25
enrolled students volunteered to participate in this optional study. In our sample of 16 students, 13
identified as male, three identified as female, and one did not specify gender identity information.
Participants’ ages ranged from 18 to 24 and they were primarily identified as White (n=11). The
other five participants identified as follows: Hispanic; Black; Asian; and biracial. One participant
did not specify their racial background.
Before the data was collected, the research procedures were thoroughly explained to potential
research participants. Students who volunteered to participate in the study signed a consent form

that described the potential risks of the study as well as their rights throughout the research
process. Consenting students completed a pre-activity survey, a post-activity survey, and a short
semi-structured interview after the game was submitted; reflection essays, submitted as part of
the graded assignment, were also collected with consent. All data was anonymized to maintain
confidentiality, and any potentially identifying information has been deleted from the dataset.
Participants are identified in this article by numbers instead of pseudonyms or names in the

The pre- and post-intervention surveys, administered via Google Forms, included questions re-
lated to their attitudes toward learning about ethics and games; their confidence in learning ethics
and engaging in game design; their definitions of ethics and the three ethical frameworks (e.g., util-
itarianism); their previous familiarity with Twine; and their demographic information. In the pre-
and post-intervention survey, we also asked them questions related to a brief ethics scenario, such
as what they would do. In the post-intervention survey, they were also asked questions about the
game they created, which ethical framework they used, how their game reflects the ethical frame-
work, and what they might change about the assignment to help them better learn about ethics in
the future. To conduct our analyses, separate spreadsheets with pre- and post- results were created.
A short, 5–10 minute semi-structured interview was conducted by the researchers after the post-
intervention survey was completed. These interviews were conducted with the 16 participants
via a video conferencing tool. The interview repeated some questions from the survey and asked
follow-up questions, such as about the game that they created, how they came up with the con-
cept, how it related to the chosen ethical framework, why they chose that framework, and what
they would change about the assignment. For this article specifically, we focused on participants’
responses to questions that pertained to their creative and game design processes and the mani-
festations of ethical frameworks within their game artifact. A master Google Docs document was
created with all the interviews to facilitate the coding process.
Collected data also included students’ reflection essays, in which they summarize their games’
story and mechanics, describe their purpose and intended alignment with one of the ethical frame-
works addressed in the assignment, and share results of playtesting sessions. Some participants
also described changes made to their games after receiving feedback from peers. These essays were
also organized in a single document for analysis by the researchers.
Finally, the Twine games themselves were not coded as artifacts for this study. The findings
reported in this article relate to game content, structure, and design features as derived from par-
ticipants’ own accounts of their games in the other aforementioned instruments (interview, re-
flection paper, surveys). We report what students explained that they designed and why instead
of a structural analysis of the games they produced. This investigation adds to previous findings
on students’ confidence on ethics knowledge and ethical decision-making in this intervention [da
Silva and Schrier 2026].
3.3 Coding Approach
We coded all the data described above using a general inductive approach [Thomas 2006] and
analyzed it using shared spreadsheets (Google Sheets). The unit of analysis was the coded segment,
defined as a passage of one or more sentences expressing a single idea about the participant’s game,
their design decisions, or their experience of the assignment. Segments could receive more than
one code. We applied codes wherever relevant content appeared, across all instruments in the
corpus, which allowed us to observe whether a participant’s account of their game held across
different instruments. Convergence across these collected data gave us greater confidence that a
coded feature reflected a participant’s design reasoning.

Both authors first conducted independent coding in parallel, separately looking for themes in
data associated with three randomly selected participants and, from there, created an initial set
of codes (code book). These spreadsheets contained columns for: participant numbers; excerpts of
their reflections, survey, or interview; the data source; and codes attributed to particular segments.
We then compared the codes we created in our initial separate spreadsheets and continued to revise
the code book, removing all overlaps and improving clarity and consistency, until a second and
more refined set of codes emerged, to create an updated code book. Next, we individually coded
the data for all of the participants again. Afterward, we compared our coded data with each other
and deliberated on the codes until we reached 100% agreement for the code assignments for every
excerpt. Final coded segments were then organized into a single spreadsheet. The coding process
produced 507 coded segments in total, with 96 segments coded in the pre-test, 169 in the post-
test, 105 in semi-structured interviews, and 137 in reflection essays. It is important to note that all
segments were analyzed in the same spreadsheet rather than divided by data source, though data
sources were specified in the analysis sheets.
After the list of codes was finalized, we determined the relative salience of each code by calculat-
ing the frequency of its occurrence across participants. For example, the code “game-consequence”
appeared across 15 participants, while “long-term consequences” appeared across five participants.
Finally, we grouped codes into different themes that emerged from the data analyzed. The resulting
themes are listed in Table 1.
The themes that related to the game elements and design processes were later wrapped up into
overarching categories. For instance, “choices and consequences” and “branching” were wrapped
up into an overarching category, Decision-Making Considerations. We will further discuss the over-
arching categories later in the paper.
4 Results
We found the following results to our research questions.
RQ #1: Which ethical framework(s) did the students use?
The frequencies for all of the codes are in Table 2. Almost all (n = 11) of the 16 students chose to
represent the Utilitarian framework through their game designs. To use the Utilitarian approach,
students had to think about how to create a game where a player needs to consider the conse-
quences of an action or decision, and choose the most optimific action to enhance well-being (the
greatest good for the greatest number”). One student (155) contextualized utilitarianism in their
game by using a moral dilemma: “It’s like, which person can you save who can help better the
village? Because they are in a war right now.” Another student designed their game such that you
can have a “good” ending only if you apply the utilitarian approach to the choices you make in
the game. 569 explained the concept of their game as follows: “you’re a traveler going to a planet,
but. . . they’re strictly utilitarian. So before you can get through customs, you’re put through... a
rigorous test, which will determine your eligibility to enter the planet.”
Two of the 16 total students chose to apply the Virtue Ethics approach. For example, students
needed to apply some aspect of virtue ethics, like enhancing or activating their players’ moral
wisdom, the ability to address moral complexity, or their facility with acting as virtuous role mod-
els. Or, students could think about the virtues themselves (e.g., generosity, kindness, patience), or
they could consider through the game what a virtuous person would do in a given scenario. One
student (419) approached this by explaining that in their game, “[y]our goal is to choose the most
virtuous person in regards to the context of the problem to model your own actions after.”
Two of the students chose the Kantian approach. For the Kantian approach, students needed

RQ #2: What were the genres of game story used?

RQ #3: What were some of the major themes and elements used in the game?
Students’ games incorporated a range of recurring design techniques, features, and practices that
can be organized into three overarching categories: decision-making structures, interpersonal di-
mensions, and instructional scaffolds. For instance, two of the themes, “choices and consequences”
and “branching” were wrapped up together into the category Decision-Making Considerations.
“Perspective-taking” and “Moral Dilemmas” were wrapped up together to create the overarching
category, Interpersonal Dimensions. The themes and overarching categories can be found in Table 3.
Decision-making Considerations All participants (n = 16) designed games around explicit choices
for players, which reflects Twine’s affordances for choice-based games and branching narratives.
These were often structured decision points that meaningfully shaped the story, determined out-
comes, or linked to ethical dilemmas; they were not just simple routine in-game actions, such as
picking up an object. For instance, one participant (898) said, “So like it gives you. . . you’re given
options. And then within those options, it’s obviously the right thing to do and the wrong thing
to do.” Another student (932) explained that, in their game, “[t]he player basically got to choose
what would make them happy in each situation. Each person has different values, which is why
there are so many different outcomes of the game.”
Almost all students (n = 15) also incorporated consequences that followed from those choices.
The game player would not just make a choice, but they were shown the consequence of that
choice, either in the short or longer-term. For instance, one student (49) explains that, in their
game, “your goal. . . is to make the correct decisions and rob the warehouse unharmed. Their [sic]
are multiple ways the player can win the game but their [sic] are also a lot of ways the player can
fail and he would have to restart.”
Within this category, a subset of five students extended their designs to also include long-term
consequences, where player actions produced ripple effects beyond immediate outcomes. One stu-
dent (205), who applied the utilitarian approach, explained that, in their design,

has a choice of whether to release the invention now or let Thomas decide its fate, and the ending

Thirteen students designed multiple possible outcomes, and created different endings or branch-

multiple possible outcomes, and both extended the basic structure of choices and consequences.

Interpersonal Dimensions Not surprisingly, given the “interpersonal” and “societal” aspects of
the frameworks that were shared, students incorporated some elements that related to taking on
others’ perspectives, such as by using scenarios or moral dilemmas to help illustrate others’ stories
and viewpoints. Three students designed for perspective-taking, positioning the player as a pres-
ident, CEO, or doctor whose role required making ethically charged decisions. For instance, one
student (898) said that “I just thought that, like, that’d be a fun game to play if you were in the shoes
of a president because I’m sure many people want to know how [sic] it’s like being a president.”
We use scenarios, role plays, and stories in class to learn about these three ethical frameworks,
and five students explicitly incorporated moral dilemmas that pertain to people into their games.
One student explained that, in their game, “...there’s no possible scenario where you can save
yourself and the greatest amount [sic] of people. It’s either you have to sacrifice yourself and
everybody gets to live, or everybody will die. Except for you” (199). Two students incorporated
a “Trolley Problem” into their game, though the class had not reviewed that by the point when
the game was being created. One student (24) explained, “I based [my game] on the train problem
- where you divert the train and spare a number of people and save lives but you hurt someone
else. You have more people that you spare, that gave me. . . motivation for the ending.” One student
integrated compassion and care for others explicitly as guiding design principles. The student (419)
said that, “I .. made it so that the player has to use their emotions to. . . see like what they think is
like a conflicting advice to the virtue. . . .You decided to create a research team to look into possible
solutions to the drought in Africa and the problems coming from it. This was done for the sole
reason of caring for others.” This aligns well with the virtue ethics approach, which they used.
Virtue ethics focuses in part on the use of emotions and care to make decisions, so it was useful
to see this also illustrated in the game that they developed.

them through the game. Rather, they also wanted to teach others these frameworks through their
game designs. Their techniques for doing this included reminders, nudges, and the ability to re-
visit previous choices (reflection). Unlike the decision-making and ethical dimensions, scaffolding
served as a complementary layer, reinforcing or clarifying the ethical reasoning embedded in other

RQ #4: How did the students characterize their experience with Twine?
All the students in the study were able to complete the assignment and use Twine to design their
games. Six of the 16 participants, however, mentioned that they did not feel confident with the
Twine tool even after using it for the game assignment. For the six that mentioned this, it was due
to them wanting to perform more advanced design tasks with Twine, such as adding images or
sounds, but not knowing how to do it. When asked an open-ended question about using Twine,
a student (199) explained that, “one thing that I had a difficulty with was adding pictures. . . .And I
tried to figure out how to do that and had complications there as well, because I wanted to have
like a spooky sound the whole time in the background.” While another student (659) echoed this
by saying: “I would have liked to be able to get some images in there. But I couldn’t figure that out
with Twine. I’m not particularly adept at Twine.” Students were not required or even encouraged
to include images or sounds, but they wanted to go beyond the requirements of the assignment to
create their games.
One student also mentioned that they felt constrained by the functionalities afforded by Twine.
They (569) said, “through Twine where, you know, you’re pretty limited in what you can do.” On
the other hand, the same student said there were a lot of possibilities for designing with Twine.
They explained that, “I also thought it was good, because . . . you can also add on as much as you
want to it.”
5 Discussion
Our results suggested that students created a variety of Twine games that allowed them to exter-
nalize and apply their understanding of the three ethical frameworks in ways that align closely
with constructionist principles. Constructionism emphasizes learning-by-making [Papert 1980],
and in this case, the artifact of a Twine game became a vehicle through which students trans-
lated abstract ethical concepts into concrete, shareable systems of choices and consequences. By
creating games that could be played, tested, and critiqued, students not only demonstrated com-
prehension, but also engaged in the iterative refinement of their understanding through artifact
design and peer feedback [Kafai and Burke 2015].
These findings also resonate with theories of game-based learning, particularly the emphasis
on decision-making, branching consequences, and perspective-taking as drivers of reflection and
engagement [Schrier 2015; Nadolny et al. 2020]. Twine’s affordances encouraged students to de-
sign games that included choice, consequences, and multiple outcomes, as well as dilemmas that
encouraged perspective-taking and consideration of other people’s needs. The sub-sections below
summarize key takeaways from our results.
5.1 The Assignment Was Completed within Given Constraints
All students in the study were able to complete the assignment and develop a Twine game that met
the constraints of the assignment (such as the timeframe of the assignment and the limitations of
the tool). This is despite six of the students remarking (unprompted) that they felt that their skills
with Twine were not as advanced as they hoped. These students felt that there was more that they
wanted to do with their game, even beyond what the assignment required, which also suggests that

they were meaningfully engaged in the game-making process, even if they were unable to fully
realize their vision. These findings align with Thompson [2020] report of increased engagement
as a result of using Twine for game development in an instructional setting.
Twine was also relatively quick for the students to get started with, as they were able to begin
crafting their games with only 15–20 minutes of instruction on how to use the basic elements
of the platform. Such findings indicate that the user-friendliness and simple interface of the tool,
previously identified by Friedhoff [2013], eliminated barriers that allowed students to quickly im-
plement their ideas and realize their vision for their games in an effective manner.
5.2 Choices and Consequences Mattered in the Design
All the participants in the study included choices in their game’s design, and most included out-
comes or consequences based on the choices that players made. The use of choices may be in part
due to the affordances of Twine, which encourages designers to make a branching choice-based
game where players can choose one path or another [Friedhoff 2013]. Thus, the use of conse-
quences may also have been due to how Twine is designed, and what is supported by the platform.
However, the use of choices and consequences in the game was also likely due to many of the stu-
dents designing their games around the Utilitarian framework, which relates more explicitly to the
consideration of expected outcomes from possible ethical choice paths to help figure out the most
optimific choice by calculating the greatest good that can be attained by the greatest number.
Moreover, the game activity helped students to design scenarios that involved not just cause
and effect, but ones that grappled with longer-term consequences. For instance, drawing upon
the Utilitarian framework, one student’s (24) fantasy-genre game puts the player in the shoes
of a character who needs to make choices to escape a monster in a mysterious setting with the
help of a young girl whose soul, the player finds at the end of the game, is connected to the
monster. The player eventually sees how the way they treated the monster affects not just the
near-term outcomes but the longer-term impact on a village. This relates, for instance, to work
done by Schrier [2017], where the researcher finds that playing a game, Fable III, helped players to
grapple not only with the outcomes of their possible choices, but a dynamic web of interlocking
factors, such as ones related to empathy, information, reasoning, and reflection. It also relates,
like previously shared, to the types of discussions the class had about Utilitarian approaches and
expected short- and longer-term outcomes.
Designing a system and a story world may have helped the students imagine and construct eth-
ical choices and possible outcomes. They used multiple endings and different pathways, perhaps
to help emphasize to the player that their actions and consequences have impact and weight. In
that regard, the experience allowed students to consider how important the player’s experience is
to the design process [An 2016]. For instance, one student required players to answer questions
using a Utilitarian approach to be able to get the “most optimal” ending. Thus, students used the
strengths of Twine to create a branching story or problem, where one’s decisions mattered and
different pathways were possible. These findings highlight students’ efforts in designing meaning-
ful choices to engage their players [Nadolny et al. 2020; Schrier 2021] and promote empathy and
reflection for their players [Schrier 2017]. It shows that the students were going beyond what they
needed to understand about the frameworks and started to consider what their players might need
to be able to understand the frameworks.
5.3 The Design Externalized Their Thinking
Students also used their game designs as a way to talk about and reflect on the ethical frame-
works themselves. The didactic and discussion-based instruction prior to the game’s design as-
signment included an evaluation of the three frameworks, such as their perceived strengths and

weaknesses. However, being able to visualize and experience these strengths and weaknesses first-
hand throughout the design process seemed to be particularly beneficial for students, and seemed
to help them bridge the gap between theory and practice through game design [Frydenberg 2015;
Roine et al. 2021].
In their interviews about their design work, three student participants commented on their own
critiques of the ethical frameworks they chose to use in their game’s designs, and the problems
that may arise from heeding too closely to those frameworks. They acknowledged that the design
process helped them to see these problems, and also to highlight them for their players. For in-
stance, one student’s (674) game “encompassed the ethical framework of utilitarianism and shows
the flaws within the way of thinking.” This example illustrates that the game design experience
helped students creatively develop and express a critical lens, which aligns with previous findings
related to the critical thinking benefits of design-based learning [Weng et al. 2023]. Participants
also invited their imagined audience to share in this critique, potentially fostering interpersonal
connections [Kafai and Burke 2015].
Being able to design a game that applies an ethical framework seemed to also help the students
to have creative control over it, and more readily construct with it, rather than be intimidated by it.
Participants’ familiarity with or interest in games and game design helped to ease them into new
concepts. Beyond just participating in the formal class discussions and readings, the game activity
helped to extend their thinking and continue their practice in understanding and grappling with
these ethical frameworks.
6 Final Considerations
This study investigated the potential of Twine as a pedagogical tool for ethics education. Specif-
ically, we examined how students used Twine to design games that applied ethical frameworks
and what this process revealed about their reasoning. Our results indicate that the chosen devel-
opment platform, Twine, facilitated participants’ realization of their designs due to its simplicity
and user-friendliness [Friedhoff 2013]. The shallow learning curve allowed students to focus on
contextualizing their chosen ethical framework in a designed game that often included choices,
branching options, and consequences to players’ actions, which effectively communicated their
understanding in a designed, playable system (rather than what is typically used for assessment,
like a paper or oral exam). For most participants, the Utilitarian framework was chosen as the inspi-
ration for their games, possibly due to its alignment with branching narratives or moral dilemmas
that students could easily apply to their Twine game design, such as the trolley problem.
There are several limitations of the study. First, our sample size was limited to 16 participants,
so this exploratory design intentionally privileged depth over breadth. The qualitative richness of
student reflections and game artifacts provided insight into how Twine supported the contextual-
ization and application of ethical frameworks, even if generalizability is not claimed. The sample
is also limited by the use of a specific class at a private regional university in the United States
for students who are not majoring in Philosophy or Ethics. Furthermore, we could not control for
similarities or differences across students’ understandings of the frameworks, and whether they
had or had not previously taken other ethics courses. Likewise, we could not control for the level
of expertise with Twine and previous knowledge of game design. Finally, since this study adopted
a qualitative approach, it is likely that researchers’ subjectivities could have influenced the codes
and themes that were extracted from the data provided.
This study provides initial evidence that game design with Twine can help students external-
ize, apply, and critique ethical frameworks. Ethics is a particularly complex construct because it
requires learners to grapple with ambiguity, weigh multiple perspectives, and consider the conse-
quences of actions. However, these are types of processes that game development platforms, and

particularly, Twine’s branching structure, may help to facilitate. Unlike other domains where an-
swers may be more straightforward, ethics education can benefit from a design-based approach

larger, controlled sample size. We also encourage researchers to investigate how specific aspects
of the game design process, such as brainstorming and ideation, playtesting, writing choices, and
imagining and describing systems, might more specifically relate to enhancing understanding of
