Introduction to the Special Issue for the Symposium on
Algorithmic Game Theory (SAGT) 2024
The 17th International Symposium on Algorithmic Game Theory (SAGT 2024) was held in Am-
sterdam, The Netherlands, on September 3–6, 2024. SAGT provides a forum for research at the
interface of computer science, economics, and game theory, including algorithmic mechanism
design, computational social choice, equilibria and their efficiency, and matching and allocation
problems. The authors of a selection of papers presented at SAGT 2024 were invited for submission
to this special issue of ACM Transactions on Economics and Computation. The six papers collected
here were substantially revised and extended, and refereed according to the journal’s standard
process. Together, they illustrate the breadth of algorithmic game theory, combining structural and
complexity-theoretic analysis with the design and evaluation of strategic systems.
The first article, Leveraging Optimal Transport to Design Optimal Mechanisms for the Facility
Location Problem, by Gennaro Auricchio and Jie Zhang, studies the 𝑘-facility location problem on the
line in a Bayesian mechanism design setting, focusing on truthful percentile mechanisms. By relating
the problem to projection in Wasserstein space, the authors characterize the asymptotic Bayesian
approximation ratio of these mechanisms, establish convergence guarantees, and identify optimal
percentile mechanisms tailored to the distribution of agents’ locations. The article additionally
treats percentile vectors with entries at the boundary of the unit interval and extends the analysis
from social cost to ℓ𝑝-norm and maximum-cost objectives.
In Price of Anarchy in Paving Matroid Congestion Games, Bainian Hao and Carla Michini study
how matroid structure limits the inefficiency of pure Nash equilibria in symmetric congestion
games. They derive upper bounds for 𝑘-uniform and paving matroids under general classes of delay
functions, as well as refined bounds for affine delays. Their analysis uses exchange flows between
an equilibrium and a social optimum, thereby connecting matroid basis exchange with the price
of anarchy. The resulting bounds show that the combinatorial structure of paving matroids can
substantially restrict equilibrium inefficiency.
The third contribution, Discrete Single-Parameter Optimal Auction Design, by Yiannis Gian-
nakopoulos and Johannes Hahn, develops a discrete counterpart to central aspects of Myerson’s
theory of optimal auctions. Using linear-programming duality and polyhedral methods, the authors
derive virtual-welfare characterizations of revenue-optimal auctions for finite-support value distri-
butions and show that deterministic dominant-strategy incentive-compatible auctions are optimal

The article also gives an application to capacitated flow auctions on trees.
The informational resources needed to sustain strategic behavior is considered next in Playing
Repeated Games with Sublinear Randomness, by Farid Arthaud. The article studies the entropy
required for Nash equilibria in finitely repeated normal-form games when randomization is scarce
or costly. It gives a complete characterization of games whose repeated versions admit constant-
randomness equilibria and proves a zero–one law: a repeated game either admits equilibria using
𝑂(1) randomness or every equilibrium requires randomness growing linearly with the number of
repetitions. The paper further characterizes attainable payoff profiles and investigates settings with
observable mixed strategies and with bounded randomness for only a subset of players.
In Structural and Algorithmic Results for Stable Cycles and Partitions in the Roommates Problem,
Frederik Glitzner and David Manlove investigate stable partitions, a solution concept that exists
even when an instance of Stable Roommates has no stable matching. The authors establish new
structural results for stable cycles and partitions, develop efficient enumeration algorithms, and
show how stable partitions correspond to stable half-matchings. They also adapt several fairness
and optimality criteria from stable matchings to stable partitions, identifying the tractable and
intractable variants and giving approximation results for some of the latter.
Finally, Estimating the Expected Social Welfare and Cost of Random Serial Dictatorship, by Ioannis
Caragiannis and Sebastian Homrighausen, addresses the evaluation of random serial dictatorship in
assignment problems. The authors consider both value-based and metric-cost settings and show that
sampling a relatively small number of priority orderings is sufficient to approximate, respectively,
the expected social welfare and expected social cost of random serial dictatorship. These results
provide computationally practical estimators despite the #P-hardness of exact evaluation.
